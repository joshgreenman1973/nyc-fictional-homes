How the addresses were found and graded

Every home on this map comes with a source you can open and a short quotation from it that supports the location. Where a work is in the public domain, the quotation is from the work itself. Otherwise it comes from the publisher, the creator, an encyclopedia entry, a script service or a press account. Homes that could not be backed up this way were left off.

The map covers the five boroughs only. Gatsby's West Egg, the Long Island and Westchester suburbs and New Jersey are out, which is why the Barones of Everybody Loves Raymond (Lynbrook) and the Petries of The Dick Van Dyke Show (New Rochelle) are not here. So are real people, even when a novel or film fictionalizes them under their own names. A home means where someone lived: a flat, a house, a boarding house, a hotel suite they kept, a museum they hid in. Workplaces and one-night scenes are not counted.

Addresses were matched to the map with the city planning department's free address search and, for street corners and named buildings, OpenStreetMap. Each point was then checked against borough boundaries, and anything that landed in the wrong borough was fixed by hand.

How precise each mark is. The shape of each mark says how much the source actually pins down.

Manhattan renumbered some streets in the 19th century, and many of the buildings named here are gone. A point marks where an address falls on today's map. It is not a claim that the building still stands.

Television and film often give a home one address and show a different building, sometimes one in Los Angeles. Where both are known, the map draws both and connects them with a thin line.
